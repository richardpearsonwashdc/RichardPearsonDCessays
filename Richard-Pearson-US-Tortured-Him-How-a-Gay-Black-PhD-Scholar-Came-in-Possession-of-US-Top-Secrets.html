US Tortured Him: How a Gay Black PhD Scholar Came in Possession of US Top Secrets Richard Pearson is a gay Black man with a completed doctorate. He has no academic appointment. He lives in Washington, D.C. For more than a year he has produced a running, multi-modal, multi-network record of events attributable to a coordinated campaign of torture, surveillance, acoustic and directed-energy harassment, physical assault, covert entry, and institutional deflection. The density of that record, the formal psychiatric clearance it contains, and the technical specificity of what he describes have placed in public view operational details that official institutions have never acknowledged. If the account is substantially accurate, the methods used against him are not the improvised work of private actors. They require sustained access to private residences, real-time indoor location awareness, the ability to deliver directional auditory effects into a human skull in a public space in the capital, the capacity to maintain months of calibrated acoustic disruption through floors, and the coordination of patterned human activity across jurisdictions-all while leaving almost no conventional forensic trail that local police or mental-health systems are equipped or willing to examine. Capabilities of that description sit at the edge of what is publicly confirmed about domestic authorities. Documenting them in real time, with timestamps, photographs, physical artifacts, and controlled geographic experiments, is therefore equivalent to placing restricted operational knowledge into the open. The US intelligence and national security apparatus caused Pearson to be in possession of top secret weapons, programs, and tactics. The sequence Pearson records begins with low-level attrition and escalates. A bicycle is progressively degraded at a Metro station. An apartment is entered without forced entry; a magnetically secured power button is removed from a kitchen appliance and left in a laundry pile. Through-floor noise tracks his movements room by room for months, producing documented sleep deprivation severe enough to drive him outdoors at night. On July 13, 2025, near the National Mall, a directional female voice enters his head, carrying ambient sounds consistent with a live operations space, instructs him to go to Georgetown, and tells him, "We have to make you disappear." He records crying openly in the street. Later he maps sharp, location-specific clicks against lamp posts, vehicles, and people with handheld devices. In December 2025 he is physically assaulted, sustains visible facial injuries, and is himself arrested after police decline to act against the person he identifies. Throughout, red-and-black clothing and objects appear on his routes in concentrations he photographs and timestamps. In September 2025 the District of Columbia Department of Behavioral Health evaluates him and finds him rational, coherent, and free of persistent psychotic symptoms. No technical investigation follows. A person who is simply ill does not generate a file of this structure while remaining functional enough to maintain it. A system confident that the claims are delusional does not need to work so carefully to avoid examining them. The combination-dense contemporaneous documentation, a formal clearance of psychosis, and sustained institutional non-response-creates an evidentiary problem that ordinary dismissal cannot cleanly resolve. The more coherent the record becomes, the more the refusal to investigate itself becomes part of the observable pattern. What Pearson has therefore come to possess is not a set of classified documents in the conventional sense. It is operational knowledge derived from direct exposure. He has recorded the signature of a method that fuses technical effects with human street theater, that prioritizes functional destruction over formal prosecution or physical elimination, and that appears designed to remain deniable precisely because individual incidents look coincidental or psychiatric when viewed in isolation. By assembling the aggregate in public, with metadata and controlled observations, he has forced into view practices whose existence official language still treats as speculative or foreign. That is the sense in which a gay Black PhD scholar without an institutional post, still living in the capital, has come into possession of material that functions as top-secret knowledge: the detailed, time-stamped description of how such a campaign is actually run against a citizen on domestic soil. The implications extend beyond one man. If the methods are real, then the boundary between foreign intelligence tools and domestic control tools has already been crossed in practice. Formal oversight mechanisms-courts, inspectors general, conventional journalism, mental-health evaluation-have shown themselves poorly matched to effects that leave little conventional residue and that can be framed as the target's pathology. Legal doctrines of sovereign immunity, classification, and standing further insulate the activity. The result is a form of power that can be exercised against selected individuals while the larger constitutional surface remains intact for everyone else. Pearson's file does not prove every technical claim to the standard a courtroom would require. Independent forensic examination of artifacts, instrumented measurement during claimed events would still be necessary. What the file does establish is that a high-density, mid-operation record produced by a cleared subject has been met with silence rather than investigation. That silence, repeated across agencies, is itself evidence of either profound institutional incapacity or deliberate avoidance of a category of claim the system is not prepared to open. A gay Black scholar with a doctorate, no university appointment, and the persistence to keep documenting has therefore done something institutions did not anticipate. He has made the operational signature visible. In doing so he has placed in public hands knowledge of methods whose official non-existence is part of their design. The country should treat that knowledge as a demand for accountability.
