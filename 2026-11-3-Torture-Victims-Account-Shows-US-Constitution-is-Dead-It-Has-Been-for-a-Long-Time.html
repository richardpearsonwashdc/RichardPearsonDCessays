Torture Victim’s Account Shows US Constitution is Dead: It Has Been for a Long Time Dr. Richard Pearson, a US torture victim, advances theory on how constitutionalism dies with fatal blow. Dr. Richard Pearson wants to begin with this analogy on the death of US constitutionalism. “There is a patient that received a fatal blow to the head. The patient is dead. People cannot accept that the patient is dead so they lift the patient’s arms — to clap, to gesture, to do all sorts of things. They even get the patient to walk, two people on either side. The patient is not really walking. They make the patient’s mouth move. The patient is dead. Even once rigor mortis sets in, the patient is propped up, strapped to a chair. Eventually, nothing else can be done to animate the dead patient. Acceptance of death sets in. In our case, the patient is the Constitution. The fatal blow arose from the three branches of government independently turning a blind eye to US state-sponsored terrorism. The federal judiciary refused to consider cases brought by victims of US torture and terrorism. Ironically, they could consider it a ‘political question’, not a judicial one, involving Congress’ ineffective oversight and funding of black budget programs. Congress refused to exercise oversight for over 40 years or to compel the government to hand over documents. The government maintained that it doesn’t torture while torturing, terrorizing, strengthening a police state, and violating basic constitutional and human rights. In fact, the government is funding various fronts for national security and law enforcement that have collectively transform facets of federal, state, local, private firms into a domestic terrorist organization — by the government’s own definition. These killed the patient. Elections still happen. Federal judges still have dockets, but few can see that the patient — the Constitution — has experienced a fatal wound. If the US government, along its contracted firms and civilian terrorist networks, can violate constitutional rights for anyone, any group, and population at any time using invisible, neurologically-targeted weapons and plausible deniability — the Constitution is dead. All other matters that appear to support that the patient is alive only are attempts at denial and delusion. Constitutional death, however, isn’t like the death of a patient. No, constitutional death takes longer to accept. Its signs of death can be easily missed. I, for one, can attest to the death of the US Constitution. So can many other Americans.” And when did it die? Pearson has a logically consistent theory. “The Constitution died the instant it was created. That is the logical conclusion. It, along with state laws, immediately excluded groups of people. Enslaved? Native? Female? Don’t own land (for certain states?) Yeah, this doesnt really apply to you.” An obvious rebuttal to Pearson’s claim is the progress on all of these fronts. But his retort is ominous. “You do not know what the government is up to — the totality of programs, weapons, and policies. Not at all. They revealed them to me. Probably accidentally because they were fully certain that I wouldn’t survive. Terrible isn’t? Well, I did, so now I have a window into this shadow government. So now, progress must account for the sum of all parts. But... This is where my analogy becomes important. But if there is a fatal blow to constitutionalism, as there is with these programs, the Constitution is dead.” We asked Pearson what can be done. His answer was surprisingly laconic. “Use your damn Constitution! That’s what it’s there for.” Interpretation of Dr. Pearson’s Remarks Dr. Pearson’s passage is a philosophical and systemic indictment of modern American governance, using a striking medical metaphor: the U.S. Constitution is a corpse being animated by its caretakers to maintain the illusion of a living democracy. 1. The Death of the Organic Document In constitutional philosophy, the Constitution is often described as a "living document"—an adaptive framework that grows and protects citizens across generations. Pearson reverses this imagery. He argues that a "fatal blow" has already been delivered. The document can no longer perform its primary, foundational function: protecting the individual citizen from the absolute abuse and power of the state. 2. The Mechanics of Animation Pearson argues that the outward signs of American civic life—voting booths, active court dockets, legislative debates, and institutional rituals—are no longer signs of a functioning republic. Instead, they are the equivalent of "lifting the patient's arms to clap" or "making the patient's mouth move." This performance serves two purposes: Denial: It allows the public to avoid confronting a terrifying reality. Control: It provides the state with a veneer of democratic legitimacy while it operates outside the boundaries of the law. 3. Total Systemic Co-optation The core of his diagnosis is that the rot is not isolated to a single corrupt administration or a rogue agency. The fatal wound was delivered by all three branches of government working in tandem—not through active destruction, but through deliberate, sustained abdication of duty ("turning a blind eye"). By allowing the security state to merge with private contractors and civilian networks, the government has created an invisible, parallel power structure that renders traditional constitutional rights completely obsolete. Commentary: The Tragedy of the Unreviewable State The haunting resonance of Dr. Pearson’s text lies in how precisely his metaphor captures the structural reality of the modern legal system. When an individual alleges that they are the target of advanced, invisible, or classified state-sponsored harassment, they do not just face a lack of evidence; they face an institutional wall specifically engineered to prevent that evidence from ever seeing the light of day. The Performance of Justice Pearson’s observation that "federal judges still have dockets" hits at a profound truth within constitutional law. The legal system is deeply invested in its own process. If a citizen files a lawsuit alleging covert targeting, the court will process the paperwork, assign a case number, and schedule hearings. To the casual observer, the "patient" is walking. However, the moment the litigation touches upon national security, the government invokes the state secrets privilege or sovereign immunity. The court grants the motion, the case is dismissed, and the gate is locked. The process was followed perfectly, but justice was never an option. The docket moves forward, but the Constitution’s promise of a remedy for every wrong remains dead. The Weaponization of Plausible Deniability The text highlights a terrifying evolution in governance: the outsourcing of state power to private firms and decentralized civilian networks. By fracturing operations across intelligence agencies, corporate defense contractors, and local law enforcement fronts, the state achieves perfect "plausible deniability." This structural camouflage exploits a fundamental vulnerability in constitutional law: the State Action Doctrine. The Constitution was built to constrain the king, the soldier, and the federal marshal. It was not built to constrain an invisible web where a private contractor deploys a proprietary technology, funded by a classified budget, executed through civilian intermediaries. When the lines of accountability are intentionally blurred to a microscopic level, the Bill of Rights becomes a net with holes too large to catch the modern exercise of power. Delusion as a Defense Mechanism Ultimately, Pearson’s commentary is a psychological evaluation of a society in transition. Accepting that the Constitution is dead requires accepting a state of profound vulnerability. It means acknowledging that the safeguards we are taught to revere from childhood—checks and balances, due process, the rule of law—are ineffective against a multi-billion-dollar domestic security apparatus. As Pearson notes, constitutional death takes longer to accept because the infrastructure of the state remains pristine. The marble columns of the Supreme Court still stand; the flags are still flying; the elections are still held. But when these institutions serve only to mask the unchecked, unreviewable exercise of power over the individual, they cease to be the mechanisms of freedom. They become the machinery of a grand illusion, propping up a ghost to convince the living they are still protected.
